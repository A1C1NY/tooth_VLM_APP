# Outcomes reporting in systematic reviews on non‐surgical root canal treatment: A scoping review for the development of a core outcome set

**Authors:** Lise‐Lotte Kirkevang, Ikhlas A. El Karim, Henry Fergus Duncan, Venkateshbabu Nagendrababu, Casper Kruse

## Background

Studies related to non‐surgical root canal treatment are amongst the most frequently performed clinical studies in endodontics. However, heterogeneity in reporting outcomes and lack of standardization is a significant challenge to evidence synthesis and guideline development.

## Objectives

The aims of the present scoping review were to (a) identify outcomes reported in systematic reviews evaluating non‐surgical root canal treatment; (b) identify how and when the reported outcomes were measured; (c) assess possible selective reporting bias in the included studies. The information obtained in this study should inform the development of a core outcome set (COS) for non‐surgical root canal treatment.

## Methodology

Structured literature searches were performed to identify systematic reviews on non‐surgical root canal treatments published in English between January 1990 and December 2020. Two reviewers undertook study selection and data extraction. Outcomes were categorized according to a healthcare taxonomy into five core areas (survival, clinical/physiological changes, life impact, resource use, and adverse events). The outcome measurement tools and length of follow‐up were recorded.

## Results

Seventy‐five systematic reviews were included, of which 40 included meta‐analyses. Most reviews reported on physiological and clinical outcomes, primarily pain and/or radiographic assessment of periapical status, and a variety of measurement tools and scales were used. Few reviews focused on tooth survival, life impact, resources, and adverse events. The heterogeneity amongst the reviews was large on all parameters. Less than 40% of the reviews assessed the risk of selective reporting.

## Discussion

Overall aims of the included reviews were highly heterogenic; thus, outcomes and how they were measured also varied considerably. Patient‐centred outcomes and the use of resources were rarely reported on.

## Conclusions

Most studies reported on physiological and clinical outcomes, in particular pain and/or radiographic healing. Measurement tools, scales, thresholds, and follow‐up periods varied greatly within each outcome, making comparison across studies complicated. Less than 40% of the reviews assessed risk of selective reporting; thus, selective bias could not be ruled out. The presented information on reported outcomes, measurement tools and scales, and length of follow‐up may guide the planning of future research and inform the development of a COS for non‐surgical root canal treatment.

## INTRODUCTION

The aim of non‐surgical root canal treatment is to prevent or treat apical periodontitis and is at the centre of the discipline of endodontics. Numerous studies have been undertaken to improve and refine all steps in the treatment, from diagnosis, access preparations, chemo‐mechanical disinfection, to restoration of the root filled tooth (Kirkevang & Væth, 2019 ).

The primary aim of a systematic review is to retrieve and synthesize available information on a specific treatment modality using strict methodological parameters and preferably perform a meta‐analysis in which the combination of results from individual studies is used to increase the statistical power and precision of the effect estimate of a given intervention (Clarke & Williamson, 2016 ). The opportunities for doing quality meta‐analysis are, however, often limited due to heterogeneity within the reported outcome measures from the included studies. Hence, many systematic reviews are confined to summarizing the reported findings of the included studies. To homogenize the reporting of outcomes amongst studies, establishing a minimum set of standardized outcomes would enable researchers to more easily combine and compare research results within a subject (Adams et al., 2013 ). Such a standardized set is referred to as a core outcome set (COS), defined as a standardized collection of the minimum outcomes that should be measured and reported for a specific area of health (Clarke & Williamson, 2016 ). COS may increase the likelihood that important outcomes are measured, improve evidence synthesis by reducing heterogeneity between studies, and reduce outcome‐reporting bias to improve the validity of the study (Clarke, 2008 ; El Karim et al., 2022 ).

In the field of endodontics, clinical studies have traditionally focused on clinician‐reported outcomes, which are measured by clinical and/or radiographic examination, whereas patient‐reported outcomes are less frequently employed (Duncan et al., 2021 ). Unfortunately, the lack of standardization between studies, for example in radiographic evaluation criteria, have limited the possible benefits from evidence synthesis in systematic reviews and meta‐analyses (Cushley et al., 2019 ; Cushley et al., 2021 ; El Karim et al., 2022 ; Wu et al., 2009 ). As part of the process of establishing a COS for endodontic treatment, an identification and thorough review and analysis of previously employed measures and outcomes are needed.

Endodontics encounter different treatment modalities: non‐surgical root canal treatment, surgical endodontic treatment, vital pulp treatment, and regenerative endodontic therapy. Since the aims and outcomes of different treatment modalities vary, separate scoping reviews have to be performed for each treatment modality (El Karim et al., 2021 , 2022 ).

The aims of the present scoping review were to: Identify outcomes reported in systematic reviews evaluating non‐surgical root canal treatment. Identify how and when the reported outcomes were measured. Assess possible selective reporting bias in the included studies.

The information obtained in this study should inform the development of COS for non‐surgical root canal treatment.

## METHODS

### Protocol registration and reporting guidelines

A protocol for the Development of Core Outcome Sets for Endodontic Treatment modalities (COSET) was developed and registered in COMET (El Karim et al., 2021 ). The COSET protocol was based on COS‐STAP Statement (Kirkham et al., 2016 ). The methodology followed Core Outcome Sets for Endodontic Treatment modalities (COSET), and the report followed PRISMA‐ScR guidelines (Page et al., 2021 ; Tricco et al., 2018 ).

### Selection criteria

The present review focused on non‐surgical root canal treatment. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in Table 1 .

### Literature search

Structured literature searches were performed by two reviewers (LLK, CK) using PubMed, Embase, OVID, Web of Science, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. Systematic reviews published between January 1990 and December 2020 were identified. Depending on the database and search engine keywords, MeSH terms and filters were used. Keywords included the following: endodontic, endodontics, root canal treatment, root canal therapy, root canal filling, pulpectomy, pulpectomies, root canal obturation, root canal retreatment. All retrieved studies were imported to Endnote 20 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA) and thereafter moved to Covidence (Covidence, Melbourne, Australia) for reviewers´ assessment. Identification of duplicates was performed by specific tools in Endnote and Covidence. All identified duplicates were reviewed, and removal was approved by hand if appropriate. Title and abstract screening and full‐text review were performed individually by both reviewers (Figure 1 ). Any disagreement about article inclusion was resolved by discussion. The detailed search strategy is presented in Table S1 .

### Data extraction and categorization into domains

Two reviewers (LLK, CK) were involved in data extraction, and any disagreement was resolved by discussion. For the selected reviews, data were extracted using bespoke data extraction forms. The data extraction form was piloted on three papers and modified as necessary before use. For each included review, the following information was retrieved: First author, publication year, country of corresponding author, journal name, aim/research question, outcomes, meta‐analysis (yes/no). The data were further classified into core areas using the health taxonomy described by (Dodd et al., 2018 ) and included the following: tooth survival, clinical and physiological changes, life impact, use of resources and adverse effects. Within these core areas, the outcomes were further grouped into a number of outcome domains (Table 2 ).

A limited number of systematic reviews reported on tooth survival ( n = 8) (Table 4 ), life impact ( n = 5) (Table 6 ), resource use ( n = 19) (Table 7 ) and adverse effects ( n = 5) (Table 8 ). The main proportion of the systematic reviews reported on physiological and clinical outcomes ( n = 66), primarily pain ( n = 28), and radiographic assessment of periapical status ( n = 39) or a combination of both clinical and radiographic measures ( n = 18) (Table 5 ).

Pain was most often assessed by use of a pain scale and the patient history. There was marked variation in the type of measurement tools and thresholds for pain, including various versions of the Visual Analogue Scale; measuring on scales from 1–9 to 0–170, and Verbal Rating Scale or Numerical Rating Scale; measuring on 2–10 point scales. Furthermore, in many studies different combinations of pain measurements and radiographic assessments were used as the reported outcome measures. For pain, the follow‐up period varied considerably from <1 day up to more than 1 year. Most studies were, however, reporting pain up to 7 days after treatment (Table 5 ).

Radiographic assessment of periapical status was generally performed using periapical radiography. The radiographic criteria used for assessment varied considerably, as did the length of follow‐up. The measurement tool most often used for radiographic follow‐up was the Periapical Index (Ørstavik et al., 1986 ) applied in 12 studies, followed by ‘radiolucency or not’ ( n = 11). The radiographic criteria for healing by Rud et al. ( 1972 ) were applied in several studies on non‐surgical endodontic retreatment ( n = 9) even though the criteria originally were developed to assess outcome in relation to surgical endodontic retreatment. Some studies reported on radiographic outcome after a follow‐up period of 6 months, but most studies reported after 1–4 years. Panoramic radiographs were rarely used ( n = 2). Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) was used in a few studies ( n = 6), but the disease criteria varied considerably, and the follow‐up periods varied between 1–4 years (Table 5 ).

The clinical parameters swelling, sinus tract, tenderness to percussion or palpation, periodontal pockets, and mobility of the tooth were reported infrequently, and with considerable variation in assessment tool and criteria. Furthermore, the follow‐up period varied. Swelling and sinus tract were primarily assessed within the first weeks or months after treatment, whereas the other clinical parameters were assessed after longer follow‐up period of 1 year or more (Table 5 ).

Bacterial reduction was assessed in 18 studies (Table 5 ). The most common feature amongst these studies was that they reported on very short follow‐up periods, primarily ranging from before the start of root canal treatment and until root filling procedure. Only a few studies ( n = 4) reported on effect of bacterial presence at time of root filling on long‐term follow‐up (up to 4 years).

The effect of non‐surgical root canal treatment on the patient's quality of life was reported on less often ( n = 5). A few studies ( n = 3) reported on short‐term discomfort or fatigue, restricted to the days immediately after the treatment had been performed. Only one study reported on patients’ need for sick leave in relation to the treatment. Within the few included studies, the outcome measure varied considerably (Table 6 ).

More studies ( n = 19) reported on need for medication, primarily if the patient had need of analgesics. The follow‐up ranged from 1 day to 1 year or more (Table 7 ). In addition, adverse effects were reported in a few studies, in form of exacerbation, with a follow‐up of 2 days to 2 months, and gastro‐intestinal problems, which were reported in two studies, with a short follow‐up of a few days (Table 8 ).

Of the 75 included reviews, 29 assessed selective reporting bias in the included studies, 17 of these assessed all studies to have a low risk of selective reporting bias and few studies were assessed as being in high risk of selective reporting bias. The overall risk of bias in the studies often was assessed as moderate to high (Table 9 ).

## DISCUSSION

The present scoping review aimed to provide an overview of outcomes, outcome measures, measurement tools and follow‐up periods used in studies on non‐surgical root canal treatments. The collected information should be used to inform the development of a COS for non‐surgical root canal treatment.

It was shown that even though the included systematic reviews all report on outcomes related to the same treatment modality, they most often posed different research questions with focus on different aspects of treatment, and consequently, they reported using a variety of outcomes, measurement tools, and follow‐up periods. However, variation in research questions was not the entire explanation of the heterogenic outcomes, since even studies with similar aims chose to report outcome differently. This is also reflected in the fact that in only 40 out of 75 (53%) included systematic reviews meta‐analysis was performed.

In the present scoping review, 18 outcome domains within five core areas were identified (Table 2 ). In a recent study, focusing on non‐surgical root canal treatment, non‐surgical retreatment, and apexification procedures, an almost similar number of outcome measures were identified ( n = 19) (Azarpazhooh, Sgro, et al., 2022 ).

The outcomes most often reported on were within the physiological and clinical core area. The outcome measures being primarily pain and radiographic healing, and in many studies, combinations of pain measurements and radiographic assessments were used. This approach has been integrated in some of the measurement tools, for example Bender's criteria (Bender et al., 1966 ; Smith et al. 1993 ; Table S3 ), but with varying description of both the radiographic and the pain assessments. These combinations of clinician‐reported outcomes and patient‐reported outcomes may be as regarded very clinically relevant, but may also further complicate comparisons amongst studies.

For pain, the follow‐up period often was relatively short, up to 1 week after treatment, whereas longer follow‐up periods were less frequent. For radiography, the follow‐up period most often was longer, ranging from 6 months to more than 4 years.

Few studies reported on the other defined core areas: tooth survival, life impact, resource use, and adverse events. Tooth survival is probably the most important outcome from a patient's perspective. However, few studies report on this parameter as the primary outcome. Similarly, few studies report on the effect of a root canal treatment on the patient's quality of life, even though this also must be considered highly relevant for, and by, the patients. A recent scoping review looking at original studies reported similar findings, that clinical and radiographic outcomes were frequently reported, whereas patient‐centred outcomes were reported less frequently (Azarpazhooh, Sgro, et al., 2022 ). The lack of focus on the patient perspective is problematic. Therefore, it is important to include patient representatives in the development of a COS (El Karim et al., 2021 ).

In the included systematic reviews, traditional outcome measures (e.g. bacterial count, periapical imaging) were reported on more often, compared to more advanced diagnostics methods (e.g. biomarkers, CBCT). This trend was also shown in a recent scoping review reporting on four decades of outcomes in non‐surgical root canal treatment (Azarpazhooh, Sgro, et al., 2022 ). Obvious explanations for this are related to differences in time‐period and availability of the newer, more advanced methods. Care should therefore be taken not to overestimate the importance of high numbers of studies reporting on a specific outcome measure, and care should also be taken to ensure that relevant information from historic research is translatable, if a newer methodology for outcome assessment is endorsed.

Non‐surgical root canal treatment consists of many steps, for example diagnosing, obtaining anaesthesia, assess preparation, locating root canals, irrigation, and each step should be performed in an optimal manner in order to obtain an optimal prognosis for the tooth and the patient. When performing a randomized controlled trial, one parameter is investigated whilst maintaining all other variables constant. This, of course, results in a huge variety of outcome measures, which all may independently affect the outcome of the treatment. However, it is of importance to also report on the overall treatment outcome, in particular viewed from the patients' perspective. The patient‐centred outcomes are relevant both from the patients' point of view and for society and healthcare planning. Development of COSET would hopefully increase awareness and reporting on these domains in the future.

### Strengths of review

The present review provides a broad overview of reported outcomes and outcome measures in studies related to non‐surgical root canal treatment. A transparent and reproducible methodology was employed throughout the literature search, study selection, and data extraction. The review adopted a medical research taxonomy to summarize outcome into a format compatible with core outcome set development (Dodd et al., 2018 ).

### Limitations of review

Literature search was limited to systematic reviews published in English. This was done for practical reasons and may have excluded research published in other languages. The scoping review does not perform critical appraisal of neither the original studies nor the systematic reviews. Thus, the problem of selective reporting bias may be more extensive, as selective reporting may have occurred both in the original study, in the systematic review, and eventually in the present scoping review (Kirkham et al., 2010 ). Furthermore, missing data and/or misreporting of data may have increased in systematic reviews, as outcome details from the original studies, for example follow‐up time, outcome thresholds, may have been adjusted or left out, to fit the aim of a specific systematic review. A scoping review, per definition, does not perform critical appraisal of neither the original studies nor the systematic reviews. Thus, selective reporting bias may have occurred both in the original study, in the systematic review, and eventually in the present scoping review (Azarpazhooh, Cardoso, et al., 2022 ).

### Future directions

Information from the present study will be used in Phase 2 of the development of COSET, in the consensus process where the Delphi methodology and semi‐structured interviews will be used to agree on the most important outcomes to define a COS for non‐surgical root canal treatment (El Karim et al., 2021 ). Until this is developed, the researcher will have to continue to identify the outcomes used previously and individually determine what is best suited for their specific research question. Hopefully, this review may guide researchers in that respect. Furthermore, this review points at the necessity to include more patient‐reported outcomes, such as tooth survival, life impact, and adverse effects.

## CONCLUSION

The present scoping review identified outcomes related to non‐surgical root canal treatment in five core areas: tooth survival, physiological and clinical outcomes, life impact, use of resource, and adverse events. Most studies reported on physiological and clinical outcomes, in particular assessment of pain and/or radiographic healing. The measurement tools, scales, thresholds, and follow‐up periods varied greatly within each outcome, making systematic comparison across studies complicated. Less than 40% of the included reviews reported on risk of selective reporting. Consequently, selective reporting bias could not be ruled out. The presented systematic information on the reported outcomes, measurement tools and scales, and length of follow‐up may guide the planning of future research studies. Most importantly, the heterogeneity in the reported outcomes amongst studies warrants the development of a COS for non‐surgical root canal treatment.
