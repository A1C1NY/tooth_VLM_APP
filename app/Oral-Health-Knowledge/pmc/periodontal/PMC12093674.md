# Spontaneous repositioning of pathologically migratory mandibular anterior teeth following periodontal surgical treatment: a case report with 4-year follow-up and literature review

**Authors:** Yunqing Pang, Jiamin Li, Shengping Zheng, Jing Wang

Periodontal health is the foundation of both oral and general health. Consequently, the high global prevalence of periodontitis and its associated complications, such as tooth migration in moderate-to-severe cases, demand serious attention. Early detection and treatment of tooth migration are essential for favorable long-term outcomes. This article discusses a case of localized aggressive periodontitis (classified as stage III, grade C in 2018) that was managed through periodontal surgery. This intervention led to the spontaneous repositioning of the affected mandibular anterior teeth, which had undergone pathological migration. The treatment focused on eliminating occlusal trauma and inflammation by employing occlusal adjustment, subgingival scaling, and root planing (SRP), addressing both internal and external causes. Following this, alveolar bone regeneration was facilitated through guided tissue regeneration (GTR), which restored normal occlusal and maxillary balance, closed interdental spaces, and promoted the spontaneous repositioning of pathologically migrated teeth. In this instance, only periodontal treatment was applied, highlighting the importance of early and personalized interventions for pathological tooth migration (PTM). Such strategies are not only ideal but also cost-effective. Additionally, we include a literature review focusing on the etiology, therapy, and prevention of PTM associated with periodontitis.

## Introduction

Periodontal disease, a widely prevalent chronic inflammatory condition that primarily affects the periodontal supporting tissues, includes gingivitis and periodontitis. It impacts a vast majority of the global population; approximately 62% of adults are affected by periodontitis, with around 23.6% experiencing severe forms of the disease [ 1 ]. In 2019, an estimated 1.1 billion people worldwide suffered from severe periodontitis [ 2 ]. In China, the fourth national oral health epidemiological survey in 2017 indicated that approximately 62.3% of adults over the age of 35 had periodontitis, with the prevalence of severe periodontitis reaching 30.6% [ 3 ]. Predictions indicate that the incidence of periodontal disease will continue to rise over the next 25 years [ 4 ].

Severe periodontitis can lead to tooth loosening, tooth migration, and ultimately, tooth loss. Clinically, tooth migration has been diagnosed in > 30% of patients with periodontal disease [ 5 ]. This clinical manifestation is known as pathological tooth migration (PTM) [ 6 ], defined as a change in tooth position, occurring when the forces that normally maintain teeth in their positions relative to the skull are disrupted [ 7 ]. Previous research indicates that the prevalence of PTM in patients with moderate-to-severe periodontitis ranges from 55 to 72% [ 8 , 9 ]. The likelihood of PTM increases with the severity of periodontal disease and is most commonly observed in patients with severe chronic and aggressive forms of periodontitis [ 10 ].

When PTM occurs in the anterior teeth, it significantly impacts patients, causing functional and aesthetic issues that can lead to psychological distress [ 11 ]. The main treatment options for patients with periodontitis and PTM include periodontal treatment alone, combined periodontal-orthodontic treatment, and multidisciplinary approaches [ 12 ]. For severe PTM (> 1 mm), a combined treatment approach is typically adopted to achieve favorable outcomes. This article discusses a case of localized aggressive periodontitis where the teeth were preserved and treated using scaling and root planing (SRP) and guided tissue regeneration (GTR). Notably, we observed spontaneous retraction and interdental space closure of the PTM in the mandibular anterior teeth. These outcomes are rare owing to the narrow alveolar bone, thick bone cortex, and dense trabecular bone in the mandibular anterior region, compounded by the greater vertical occlusal forces exerted by these teeth at early anterior contacts. The treatment efficacy was maintained for over four years.

## Case report

A 19-year-old female patient presented with loosened mandibular anterior teeth, widened interdental spaces, and gingival bleeding for over one year. She reported gum bleeding while brushing over the past year and noted oral malodor. In the past six months, she observed that her mandibular anterior teeth had become loose, and the interdental spaces had gradually widened. Additionally, she experienced soreness and weakness during mastication, affecting her eating habits and aesthetics. The patient had not received any prior oral treatment and reported that her father had experienced a similar condition. She was otherwise healthy and denied any history of surgery, infectious diseases, or allergies to food and drugs. Her general oral hygiene practices included brushing twice daily for 2 min, using both horizontal and vertical strokes, and she had no harmful oral habits, such as smoking.

Clinical examination showed generally good oral hygiene with minor soft tartar and dental calculi on the tooth surfaces. The gingiva was slightly red and swollen, with bleeding on probing. The anterior teeth displayed an edge-to-edge occlusion, with the mandibular midline deviating approximately 1 mm to the left. Teeth 11, 12, and 42 exhibited early contact during occlusion. The gap between the central mandibular incisors was approximately 3 mm (Fig. 1 A), and the mobility of tooth 42 was approximately 1 mm. Deep periodontal pockets ranging from 7 to 10 mm in depth were probed at the mesial site of tooth 42 and the distal sites of teeth 36 and 46 (Fig. 1 B). Bleeding on probing (BOP) was positive in 17% of the sites, and 14% of the sites exhibited probing depths > 4 mm, indicating a high-risk assessment [ 13 ]. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) showed angular resorption of the alveolar bone extending to the apical third at the distal sites of teeth 36 and 46, and the mesial site of tooth 42 (Fig. 1 C). A diagnosis of localized aggressive periodontitis (periodontitis stage III, grade C, molar-incisal pattern) was made.

Initially, a two-month periodontal treatment regimen, including supragingival scaling, subgingival scaling, and root planing, was administered. Concurrently, occlusal adjustments were made: the lingual Inclined planes of teeth 11, 12 and incisal of tooth 42 were ground and modified. After this initial therapy, the BOP-positivity rate decreased to 5%, and 6% of the sites had probing depths > 4 mm (Fig. 2 A), which were classified as medium risk [ 13 ]. However, probing depths remained ≥ 7 mm at the mesial site of tooth 42 and the distal sites of teeth 36 and 46. Consequently, GTR was performed at these sites using Geistlich Bio-Oss bone graft material and Geistlich Bio-Gide biofilm to aid regeneration (Fig. 2 B and C ). Oral hygiene education was emphasized throughout the treatment, and the patient maintained good oral hygiene.

Six months postoperatively, the patient’s periodontal condition remained stable, with slight resorption of the bone graft material (Fig. 2 D). The gap between the central mandibular incisors had slightly reduced, and the irregular midline deviation had been partially corrected (Fig. 3 C). Ten months postoperatively, the gap between the mandibular central incisors had significantly decreased (Fig. 3 D). Owing to the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient was unable to attend follow-up appointments for an extended period. At 27 months postoperatively, the gap between the mandibular central incisors was completely closed, and the maxillary and mandibular midlines were aligned (Fig. 3 E). Forty-six months after the periodontal surgery, the periodontal condition was stable with no recurrence of interdental spacing. The anterior teeth exhibited a shallow overjet and overbite relationship (Figs. 3 F and 4 A). The BOP-positivity rate was 2%. Only the buccal distal site of tooth 36 was probed at a depth of 6 mm, with the remaining teeth probed at depths of ≤ 5 mm, indicating successful periodontal maintenance (Fig. 4 B and C ). ## Discussion

### Etiology of PTM accompanied by periodontitis

PTM can present in various forms, including diastema formation, rotation, extrusion, facial flaring, protrusion, and tipping into edentulous spaces, with most patients exhibiting a combination of these types [ 8 ]. Limited evidence suggests that the formation of interdental spaces in the maxillary anterior teeth and the extrusion of mandibular anterior teeth are the most common manifestations [ 8 , 14 ], whereas the formation of interdental spaces in the mandibular anterior teeth, as in this case, is less common. PTM associated with periodontitis is typically multifactorial, attributed to the destruction of periodontal supporting tissues, pressure from inflammatory tissues, occlusal factors, soft tissue pressure, and gingival hypertrophy [ 15 ]; often, more than one of these factors is present in individual cases.

Loss of periodontal supporting tissues is a critical etiological factor in PTM [ 8 , 9 , 16 ]. The likelihood of PTM increases significantly with greater bone loss, with risks ranging higher by 2.95–7.97 times [ 9 ], and teeth affected by PTM exhibit greater attachment loss and inflammation than their unaffected contralateral counterparts [ 8 , 17 ]. In this case, PTM was caused by severe periodontal bone loss in the single-rooted anterior teeth. Normally, periodontal supporting tissues contain various types of collagen fibers, primarily gingival fibers and the principal fiber bundles of the periodontal ligament, which cushion teeth against external forces and maintain their correct positioning within the dental arch [ 18 ]. However, the periodontal force around healthy teeth is not infinite. As periodontal disease progresses, structural changes in the gingiva and periodontal ligaments disrupt the force equilibrium of the teeth, ultimately leading to PTM [ 19 ].

Pressure from inflammatory tissues within periodontal pockets is another crucial factor contributing to PTM [ 15 ]. Hydrodynamic and hydrostatic forces in the blood vessels and inflammatory tissues within these pockets may also play a role in PTM [ 20 ]. During gingival inflammation, vascular permeability and blood flow to the gingiva increase, leading to elevated fluid pressure between tissues [ 15 ]. However, some case reports have noted tooth movement in the direction opposite to the deepest part of the pocket.

Occupational factors associated with the etiology of PTM include posterior bite collapse due to the loss of posterior teeth, Class II malocclusion, occlusal interference, the anterior component of force, protrusive functional patterns of mastication, bruxism, and shortened dental arches [ 15 ]. Tooth loss and arch integrity are likely the most critical occlusal factors contributing to PTM [ 15 ]. In patients with severe alveolar bone loss, these occlusal factors may become more destructive. Secondary occlusal trauma or malocclusion may develop in patients with severe periodontitis due to the loss of soft and hard periodontal supporting tissues [ 21 ]. Approximately 82.6% of patients with stage III or IV periodontitis experience occlusal trauma to the anterior teeth [ 14 ]. Severe occlusal trauma can induce periodontal destruction, increasing the risk of tooth migration [ 22 ].

Soft tissue pressure, particularly following the development of periodontal disease, can also contribute to PTM. Orthodontic studies have shown that soft tissue forces from the tongue, cheeks, and lips can influence tooth movement, especially when periodontal support is compromised [ 23 ]. Additionally, the force exerted by the tongue is often greater than the opposing forces from the lips and cheeks [ 23 ], making it more likely to cause labial tilting of the teeth. In this case, the improvement of edge-to-edge occlusion may be related to the force exerted by the labial muscles.

Gingival hypertrophy is also a contributing factor to PTM [ 15 ]. Lee et al. reported a case of advanced periodontitis complicated with type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and drug-induced gingival hypertrophy that was successfully managed with nonsurgical periodontal therapy. The favorable prognosis of PTM in this instance may have been due to a reduction in the periodontal pocket depth and gingival overgrowth rather than increased bone support [ 24 ].

Physiological tooth migration, unlike PTM, refers to the natural movement of teeth that occurs under normal physiological conditions. This movement is generally associated with the eruption of deciduous and permanent teeth, functional migrations such as occlusal adjustments, mesial drift, and age-related changes. It represents a dynamic adjustment of teeth within the oral environment, which is a normal physiological phenomenon [ 25 ]. However, if the teeth move too rapidly or excessively, or are accompanied by other symptoms, it may indicate a pathological state of tooth movement. In this case, the main factors causing PTM by periodontitis included the loss of periodontal supporting tissues, pressure from inflammatory tissues, labial inclination of the lower anterior teeth, and the resultant local occlusal trauma. Particularly notable were the rapid formation of large gaps and the loosening of teeth, which impacted feeding; these etiological factors and clinical manifestations represent a typical case of PTM.

### Therapy for PTM accompanied by periodontitis

Periodontal treatment is crucial for managing PTM associated with periodontitis. Ross et al. and Gaumet et al. used the terms “reactive positioning” or “spontaneous repositioning” to describe tooth movement that occurs after partial or complete removal of the forces causing blockage or displacement through periodontal treatment [ 26 ]. Spontaneous repositioning typically begins 7–10 days after periodontal treatment and is completed within 3–4 weeks in most cases [ 19 ]. This repositioning may result from reduced inflammatory tissue pressure [ 15 ], traction forces generated by wound contraction during healing [ 19 ], elimination of pathological granulation tissue and its generated forces [ 19 ], restoration of periodontal forces generated by periodontal fibers [ 19 ], tensile forces exerted on the tooth by the blood clot after periodontal treatment [ 27 ], root-epithelial interface by root planing [ 19 ], elimination of abnormal occlusal forces [ 28 ], and regeneration of hard and soft periodontal tissues.

Several studies have shown that periodontal treatment alone can effectively achieve closure of mild PTM (≤ 1 mm) and even larger spaces [ 29 ]. Multiple cases have reported closure of ≤ 2 mm gaps between maxillary incisors following initial periodontal therapy [ 28 , 30 – 32 ], and Sato et al. reported the spontaneous closure of a 3-mm gap between maxillary anterior teeth [ 33 ]. However, periodontal surgical treatment may yield superior results for PTM compared to nonsurgical approaches [ 19 , 34 ]. Statistically, compared to the efficacy of nonsurgical periodontal treatment over 6 weeks, the repositioning rate after 4 months of surgical treatment was 6.32% higher, and the complete repositioning rate was 24.41% higher [ 35 ]. Clinical case reports have shown that gaps of 2.5 mm and 5 mm in the maxillary anterior teeth are completely closed following periodontal flap surgery and bone grafts [ 36 , 37 ]. However, repositioning takes longer after periodontal regenerative surgery than after SRP [ 19 ]. During this period, further tooth migration may occur due to the ongoing impact of occlusion and soft tissue on the teeth [ 37 ]. Therefore, splints may be used postoperatively as an adjunct [ 28 , 33 ] to improve the stability of the treated teeth [ 38 ]. However, periodontal treatment alone has an unpredictable prognosis for PTM [ 33 ]. Although the patient’s gap was completely closed in this case by well-established periodontal initial therapy and surgical treatment, the long-term stability of the affected teeth in this study remains unclear.

For severe PTM with poor results after periodontal treatment and prolonged persistence, which are usually accompanied by severe intrabony defects, a multidisciplinary approach may be required, incorporating periodontal, orthodontic, and restorative treatments [ 15 ]. Orthodontic therapy, frequently combined with bone grafting and initiated as early as three months after surgery [ 39 ], can promote periodontal attachment [ 40 ] and improve PTM outcomes. A retrospective study demonstrated that successful outcomes could be achieved with a combination of periodontal regenerative surgery and continuous orthodontic treatment using clear aligners for stage IV grade 2 periodontitis with intraosseous defects [ 41 ]. Several case reports have indicated that combining periodontal regeneration with orthodontic treatment can significantly improve periodontal prognosis and close severe gaps [ 42 , 43 ]. Comprehensive periodontal treatment and maintenance are essential when performing orthodontic treatments. Regular periodontal tissue maintenance therapy should be performed every 2–6 months, before, during, and after orthodontic treatment [ 39 ]. Restorative therapy options include camouflage repair and post-extraction repairs [ 38 ]. Teeth that have migrated minimally or that can be retained may be restored with full crowns, veneers, or fixed bridges after periodontal inflammation has been managed and occlusal adjustments have been made to rapidly close the interdental spaces and enhance aesthetics [ 37 ]. Severely displaced and loose teeth can be addressed with implant restorations following extraction.

With the treatments described above, PTM usually results in a good or ideal prognosis, similar to the present case, where probing depths and interdental space remained stable throughout the follow-up period. However, maintaining the effectiveness of the treatment and the long-term stability of the affected teeth depends not only on adequate periodontal maintenance therapy and patient compliance but also on the patient’s commitment to maintaining oral hygiene [ 39 ]. This case is unique owing to the patient’s young age, which contributed to good periodontal vitality and resilience. Furthermore, the gradually full eruption of wisdom teeth (third molar) throughout the treatment, which might be one of the important factors contributing to the closure of the gap between the mandibular anterior teeth.

### Prevention of PTM accompanied by periodontitis

In the present case, the bacterial infection was controlled, local inflammation and occlusal trauma were eliminated, oral hygiene instructions were provided, regenerative procedures were performed, and the tooth was restored to its original position as the periodontal injury healed and the periodontal tissues regenerated. Although a good prognosis was achieved with periodontal therapy alone in this case, many severe interdental spaces still required multidisciplinary therapy, highlighting the importance of PTM prevention. Studies have shown that sites with tooth migration gaps ≤ 1 mm have the highest closure and repositioning rates [ 29 , 44 ]. The repositioning rate decreases with an increase in tooth migration gaps, underscoring the importance of early prevention and treatment of PTM. Effective control of periodontal disease is the most effective way to prevent PTM [ 15 , 37 ], and many cases of PTM can be avoided with early diagnosis and treatment of periodontal disease [ 15 ]. However, in clinical practice, individualized preventive measures and treatment plans should be developed based on a comprehensive assessment of the severity of PTM and the patient’s specific needs.

## Conclusion

This case of severe PTM is the first report to our knowledge where mandibular anterior teeth responded favorably, and the gap was successfully closed with periodontal treatment alone. The importance of standardized periodontal therapy in managing PTM due to periodontitis has been emphasized. Particularly, a proper observation period after periodontal treatment is essential, and there is no need to rush into orthodontic or restorative treatments, as this may yield exciting results. However, this case does have some limitations, including the fact that it represents an isolated instance of localized periodontitis in a young patient. In the future, digital technology, retrospective studies, and large sample analyses can be used to further investigate the etiology and prognosis of PTM in mandibular anterior teeth. Furthermore, we conducted a literature review to investigate the etiology, therapeutic approaches, and preventive measures against PTM accompanied by periodontitis.
